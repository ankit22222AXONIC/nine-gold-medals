Stanza 01 · The gathering

From every corner of the country

The athletes had come from all over the country
To run for the gold, for the silver and bronze
Many weeks and months of training
All coming down to these games.

What happens hereThe poem opens wide: athletes from across the country, months of sweat, everything riding on one day of games — gold, silver or bronze at stake.
SettingVisual imagery
Stanza 02 · The crowd

The old field fills up

The spectators gathered around the old field
To cheer on all the young women and men
The final event of the day was approaching
Excitement was high to begin.

What happens hereThe crowd circles a humble, worn ground — not a grand stadium — as the day’s final event approaches. Men and women are cheered alike, and expectation hangs in the air.
AtmosphereAnticipation
Stanza 03 · The start

Nine, poised at the blocks

The blocks were all lined up for those who would use them
The hundred-yard dash and the race to be run
These were nine resolved athletes in the back of the starting line
Poised for the sound of the gun.

What happens hereNine determined athletes crouch at the starting line of the hundred-yard dash. The stanza slows time down to a single held breath — everyone waiting for the pistol.
TensionForeshadowing
Stanza 04 · The turning point

The gun, the burst, the fall

The signal was given, the pistol exploded
And so did the runners all charging ahead
But the smallest among them, he stumbled and staggered
And fell to the asphalt instead.

What happens hereThe race explodes off the line — and in a single line the poem turns. The smallest runner stumbles, staggers, and hits the track instead of finishing the charge.
Turning pointAlliterationContrast
Stanza 05 · The low point

A cry in the dirt

He gave out a cry of frustration and anguish
His dreams and his efforts dashed in the dirt
But as sure as I’m standing here telling this story
The same goes for what next occurred.

What happens hereThe boy’s cry carries his months of effort collapsing at once — dreams “dashed in the dirt”. The poet leans in like a storyteller: but as sure as I’m standing here… something extraordinary follows.
Auditory imageryIdiomStoryteller’s aside
Stanza 06 · The reversal

Eight turn around

The eight other runners pulled up on their heels
The ones who had trained for so long to compete
One by one they all turned round and went back to help him
And brought the young boy to his feet.

What happens hereEight athletes abandon their own race — months of training set aside in a heartbeat — and turn back, one by one, to lift the fallen boy. Empathy defeats competition.
Empathy in actionReversal
Stanza 07 · The walk

Nine hands, one banner

Then all the nine runners joined hands and continued
The hundred-yard dash now reduced to a walk
And a banner above that said (Special Olympics)
Could not have been more on the mark.

What happens hereThe sprint becomes a walk, nine hands joined — and the banner overhead, Special Olympics, turns out to be more prophecy than label: acceptance and inclusion, exactly as the movement intends.
SymbolismIrony of the banner
Stanza 08 · The finish

Nine gold medals

That’s how the race ended, with nine gold medals
They came to the finish line holding hands still
And a standing ovation and nine beaming faces
Said more than these words ever will.

What happens hereAll nine cross together, hands still joined, to a standing ovation. Nine golds — because on this day, kindness was the event. The closing line admits that some moments outgrow words entirely.

Poetic devices used in the poem

Each device, with its example from the poem.

Alliteration
Repeating the same sound at the start of nearby words: “he stumbled and staggered” and “his dreams and his efforts dashed in the dirt”.
Visual imagery
Words that paint a picture: “nine resolved athletes … poised for the sound of the gun”, “holding hands still” at the finish, and “nine beaming faces”.
Auditory imagery
Words that let us hear the moment: “the pistol exploded” and the boy’s “cry of frustration and anguish”.
Symbolism
The joined hands stand for solidarity, the Special Olympics banner for inclusion, and the dash “reduced to a walk” for winning that no longer matters.

The Poet

David Roth

David Roth is an American singer and songwriter. He tells stories through his songs, in warm and simple words. Nine Gold Medals was written as a song, not as a poem — which is why it reads as if someone is telling you a story.

The folk singer Tom Paxton called it “one of the best songs I’ve heard in the last 20 years”. The song tells of a race at a Special Olympics meet, where the runners stopped to help a friend who had fallen, and all nine crossed the finish line together.

Pause & predict

The smallest runner has fallen.

The other eight are still running, and the medal is there for the taking. What do they do?

They stopped — every one of them.

Eight athletes gave up a race they had trained months for and turned back, one by one, to help a friend. It is the moment the whole poem turns on.
